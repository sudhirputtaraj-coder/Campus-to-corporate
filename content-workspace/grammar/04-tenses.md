Tenses: Showing Time Clearly in Your English

Tenses tell your listener when something happens and what kind of action it is: finished, still going on, repeated, or connected to the present. A wrong tense does not just break a rule. It can change the message. "I completed the report" and "I have been completing the report" tell two different stories.

English has twelve tense forms, but you do not need to memorise twelve separate lists. Each tense is built from a time (present, past, future) and an aspect (simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous). Learn the logic once and the rest follows.

## Three questions that pick the right tense

- When did it happen: before now, around now, or after now?

- Is the action finished, still in progress, or repeated? Is its link to another moment important?

- Which clue words are in the sentence? Words such as yesterday, since, now, by the time and every day usually point to one tense.

## The twelve tenses on one page

The same sentence is shown in every tense so you can compare the changes.

| Tense | Structure | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple present | base verb (+ s/es for he, she, it) | Meera reviews the code every morning. |
| Present continuous | am / is / are + verb-ing | I am reviewing the code now. |
| Present perfect | have / has + past participle | I have reviewed the code. |
| Present perfect continuous | have / has been + verb-ing | I have been reviewing the code since noon. |
| Simple past | past form | I reviewed the code yesterday. |
| Past continuous | was / were + verb-ing | I was reviewing the code when the power went out. |
| Past perfect | had + past participle | I had reviewed the code before the meeting began. |
| Past perfect continuous | had been + verb-ing | I had been reviewing the code for two hours when you called. |
| Simple future | will + base verb | I will review the code tomorrow. |
| Future continuous | will be + verb-ing | I will be reviewing the code at 4 p.m. |
| Future perfect | will have + past participle | I will have reviewed the code by Friday. |
| Future perfect continuous | will have been + verb-ing | By Friday, I will have been reviewing the code for a week. |

Tip: Note that the future perfect continuous is will have been + verb-ing. Some charts wrongly show it as will have + past participle, which is the plain future perfect.

# Part 1: Simple Present and Present Continuous

These two tenses describe the present, but in different ways. The simple present is about what is generally true or regular. The present continuous is about what is happening now or around now.

## Simple present

- Habits and routines: Kavya goes to college by bus.

- Facts and general truths: Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius.

- Fixed timetables: The library opens at 8 a.m.

- Add -s or -es for he, she, it: she works, he watches. Use do/does for questions and negatives: Does he work here? He does not work here. After does, the verb stays in the base form.

## Present continuous

- An action in progress at this moment: The trainer is showing the slides.

- A temporary situation: I am staying with my cousin this month.

- A fixed arrangement for the near future: I am meeting the placement officer at 3 tomorrow.

## State verbs resist the continuous

Verbs of thinking, feeling, wanting and owning describe states, not actions, and are normally used in the simple form: know, believe, understand, want, need, like, love, hate, prefer, own, belong, seem. We say "I know the answer", not "I am knowing the answer". Some verbs have two meanings: She has two brothers (possession, simple) but She is having lunch (action, continuous).

Tip: Clue words: always, usually, every day, on Mondays point to the simple present. Now, at the moment, currently, these days point to the continuous.

# Part 2: Present Perfect and Present Perfect Continuous

The present perfect links the past to the present. The action may have happened earlier, but what matters is its result or relevance now. Because it is not about a finished time, it never goes with words like yesterday, last year, in 2022 or ago. For those, use the simple past.

## Present perfect: have / has + past participle

- Experience, with ever and never: Have you ever given a mock interview?

- A recent action with a result you can see: I have updated my resume. (Here it is.)

- Unfinished periods: I have written three emails today. This week has been busy.

- States continuing up to now, with for and since: She has lived in Bengaluru for two years.

## Present perfect continuous: have / has been + verb-ing

This tense focuses on an activity and its duration. It describes something that began in the past and is still going on, or has only just stopped with visible effects. They have been waiting for thirty minutes. My eyes hurt because I have been staring at the screen all day.

## Perfect or perfect continuous?

Use the plain perfect when you care about the number or the result (I have written three reports). Use the continuous when you care about the activity or how long (I have been writing reports all morning). State verbs stay simple: I have known him since college, not "have been knowing".

Tip: Since + a starting point (since 2020, since Monday). For + a length of time (for two years, for an hour).

# Part 3: Simple Past and Past Continuous

Both tenses talk about the past, but they look at it differently. The simple past states what happened. The past continuous paints the scene: what was going on at a certain moment.

## Simple past

- A finished action at a definite time: She joined the company in 2021.

- A sequence of events: He opened the file, checked the data and closed it.

- Regular verbs add -ed (worked); irregular verbs change (went, wrote, bought).

- Questions and negatives use did + base verb: Did you go? She did not submit. After did, never use the past form.

## Past continuous: was / were + verb-ing

- An action in progress at a particular time: At 11 p.m. last night, I was studying.

- An action interrupted by another one: I was giving a presentation when the power went off. (The longer action is continuous; the shorter, interrupting action is simple past.)

- Two actions happening together: While she was cooking, he was setting the table.

## Past habits

For habits that no longer continue, use used to + base verb: He used to go to school by bike. Questions use use to: Did you use to play cricket?

Tip: When introduces a short, interrupting event; while introduces the longer, ongoing action.

# Part 4: Past Perfect and Past Perfect Continuous

When you talk about two events in the past, you sometimes need to show which came first. The past perfect marks the earlier one. Think of it as "the past of the past".

## Past perfect: had + past participle

When I reached the station, the train had left. Two past events: the train leaving (first), my reaching the station (second). The earlier event takes had + past participle; the later one takes the simple past.

- Common clues: before, after, by the time, already, just, never...before.

- Reported speech: She said that she had finished. (The original words were: I have finished.)

- If the order is already clear from before or after, the simple past is often enough: After he finished, he left.

## Past perfect continuous: had been + verb-ing

This shows how long an activity went on before a past moment, or the cause of a past situation. She was tired because she had been studying all night. I had been waiting for an hour when the bus finally came.

Tip: Do not mix the present perfect into a past story. After he has finished, he left is wrong. Use After he had finished, he left.

# Part 5: Talking About the Future

English has no single future tense. We choose a form depending on what we mean: a decision, a plan, an arrangement, a prediction or a timetable.

| Form | Use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| will + base verb | instant decisions, promises, offers, predictions | The printer has stopped. I will fix it. |
| be going to | plans already made, and predictions based on evidence | Look at those clouds. It is going to rain. |
| present continuous | fixed arrangements | I am meeting the placement officer at 3 tomorrow. |
| simple present | timetables | The exam starts at 10 a.m. on Monday. |

## Future continuous, future perfect and future perfect continuous

- Future continuous (will be + verb-ing): in progress at a future time. At 4 p.m. tomorrow, we will be giving the final presentation.

- Future perfect (will have + past participle): finished before a future time. By the time you arrive, I will have completed the setup.

- Future perfect continuous (will have been + verb-ing): duration up to a future point. By December, she will have been working here for ten years.

## Time clauses use the present

After when, if, before, after, until, as soon as and unless, use the simple present, not will. Say "I will call you when I reach", not "when I will reach". Say "If it rains, we will cancel the event", not "if it will rain".

Tip: Will can also go in the main part of the sentence: If you register early, you will get a seat. The "if" part stays in the present.

# Part 6: Choosing the Right Tense: Clues and Common Errors

In real conversations, you will not have a worksheet in front of you. You choose a tense in a second. These clue words and habits will help you choose correctly almost every time.

| Clue words | Usual tense |
| --- | --- |
| every day, usually, always, on Sundays | simple present |
| now, at the moment, currently, these days | present continuous |
| ever, never, already, just, yet, so far, this week | present perfect |
| for + length, since + start (still continuing) | present perfect / perfect continuous |
| yesterday, last year, in 2022, ago | simple past |
| when, while (past interruption) | simple past + past continuous |
| before, by the time (earlier past) | past perfect |
| tomorrow, next week, soon | will / going to / present continuous |
| by Friday, by the time you come | future perfect |

## Habits that keep your English clean

- Stay consistent. In a story or a paragraph, keep one main tense unless the time really changes.

- After did, use the base form. Did you complete, not did you completed.

- Never use the present perfect with a finished time. I completed my degree in 2022.

- In reported speech, the verb usually shifts back: She said that she was busy.

- Place already and just between the helping verb and the main verb: I have already finished.

Tip: In interviews, use the simple past for finished projects (I developed a mobile app) and the present perfect for experience that is still relevant (I have worked with three programming languages).

# Quick Recap

- Simple present for habits and facts; present continuous for now and temporary situations.

- State verbs (know, like, want, own) stay in the simple form.

- Present perfect links the past to now; never use it with yesterday, ago or a past year.

- Simple past for finished events; past continuous for the scene or the interrupted action.

- Past perfect shows the earlier of two past events.

- Will for decisions and promises; going to for plans and evidence; present continuous for arrangements.

- Future perfect continuous is will have been + verb-ing.

- After when, if, before, after, until, unless, use the present, not will.

- After did and modals, use the base verb. Keep your tenses consistent.
