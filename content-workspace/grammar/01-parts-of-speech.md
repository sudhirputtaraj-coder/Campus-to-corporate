Parts of Speech: The Building Blocks of Every Sentence

Imagine a toolbox. A hammer and a screwdriver are both useful, but you cannot swap them and expect the job to go well. Words work the same way. Every word in a sentence has a job, and when a word is used for the wrong job, the sentence feels wrong even if you cannot say why.

English has eight jobs, called parts of speech: nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions and interjections. Once you can recognise them, you can fix your own mistakes, understand why a sentence is wrong, and write with more control in resumes, emails and interviews.

Prepositions are big enough to deserve their own module (Module 3), so here we focus on the other seven and show how they work together.

## Three questions that reveal any word's job

- What is this word doing in this sentence? Naming something, replacing a name, showing action, describing, joining or reacting?

- Which word does it describe, replace or connect? A word's job is always about its neighbours.

- Can I swap it with another word of the same kind and keep the sentence sensible? If yes, you have probably identified its type.

## The eight jobs at a glance

| Part of speech | Its job | Example in a sentence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noun | Names a person, place, thing or idea | The intern submitted the proposal. |
| Pronoun | Stands in for a noun | She submitted it yesterday. |
| Verb | Shows action, state or occurrence | Nisha presents on Friday. |
| Adjective | Describes a noun or pronoun | It was a clear, concise summary. |
| Adverb | Describes a verb, adjective or adverb | He answered confidently. |
| Preposition | Links a noun to the rest (time, place, direction) | We met after the seminar. |
| Conjunction | Joins words, phrases or clauses | I prepared well, but I felt nervous. |
| Interjection | Expresses a sudden feeling | Oops! That was the wrong file. |

# Part 1: Nouns: Naming People, Places, Things and Ideas

A noun is a naming word. It names a person (Kavya), a place (the library), a thing (a laptop) or an idea or feeling (honesty, stress). Nearly every sentence has at least one noun as its subject.

## Kinds of nouns

- Common nouns name general things: student, city, company. They begin with a small letter.

- Proper nouns name particular people, places, brands or days, and always begin with a capital letter: Divya, Hyderabad, Infosys, Monday.

- Concrete nouns can be seen or touched: bag, whiteboard, phone. Abstract nouns cannot: courage, teamwork, deadline pressure.

- Collective nouns name a group as one unit: team, committee, audience, batch, jury.

## Plural forms worth knowing

Most nouns add -s (laptop, laptops) or -es after s, x, ch, sh (box, boxes). A consonant plus y becomes -ies (company, companies). Some are irregular: child becomes children, person becomes people (or persons in legal writing), man becomes men and woman becomes women.

Tip: For countable and uncountable nouns (advice, information, equipment), see Part 1 of the Articles module.

| Type | Examples | Quick check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common | teacher, village, tablet | Any one of its kind |
| Proper | Karnataka, Wipro, Diwali | A particular name, capital letter |
| Abstract | honesty, freedom, stress | You cannot touch it |
| Collective | family, class, orchestra | A group acting as a unit |

# Part 2: Pronouns: Words That Stand in for Nouns

A pronoun replaces a noun so you do not have to repeat it. Without pronouns, we would say, "Rahul told Meera that Rahul would send the file to Meera." With them: "Rahul told Meera that he would send the file to her."

## Personal pronouns change their form

| Subject | Object | My/your... (before a noun) | Mine/yours... (alone) | Reflexive |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | me | my | mine | myself |
| you | you | your | yours | yourself |
| he | him | his | his | himself |
| she | her | her | hers | herself |
| it | it | its | (none) | itself |
| we | us | our | ours | ourselves |
| they | them | their | theirs | themselves |

- Use the subject form before the verb: She sent it. Priya and I attended.

- Use the object form after a verb or preposition: He called me. This is between you and me.

- Use reflexive pronouns only when the doer and the receiver are the same person: I taught myself Python. Never use them to introduce yourself (not "Myself Kiran").

- Its has no apostrophe when it means "belonging to it". It's always means "it is" or "it has".

## Other useful kinds

- Demonstrative: this and these (near), that and those (far).

- Indefinite: everyone, someone, nothing, each, both, few.

- Interrogative: who, whom, whose, which, what.

Tip: To check a pair like "Priya and I", drop the other person. You would say "I attended", not "Me attended", so "Priya and I attended" is correct.

# Part 3: Verbs: The Engine of the Sentence

Every complete sentence needs a verb. A verb can show an action (write, solve, call), a state (be, seem, belong) or something that simply happens (occur, become).

## Three jobs a verb can do

- Main verbs carry the meaning: The team delivered the project.

- Helping (auxiliary) verbs support the main verb: be, do, have, and modals such as can, should, must. She has delivered. They did not reply.

- Linking verbs connect the subject to a describing word instead of showing action: be, seem, become, look, feel, sound, smell, taste. After a linking verb we use an adjective, not an adverb. The plan sounds good, not "sounds well".

## The five forms of a verb

| Base | Past | Past participle | -ing form | -s form |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| work | worked | worked | working | works |
| speak | spoke | spoken | speaking | speaks |
| write | wrote | written | writing | writes |
| go | went | gone | going | goes |
| bring | brought | brought | bringing | brings |
| choose | chose | chosen | choosing | chooses |

Regular verbs add -ed for both the past and the past participle. Irregular verbs change their form, so these are worth learning by heart. The past participle is used after have, has, had (I have written) and in passive sentences (the report was written).

Tip: After a modal verb (can, could, should, must, will, would) always use the base form, with no -s, -ing or "to": She can speak. They should leave.

# Part 4: Adjectives: Adding Detail to Nouns

An adjective describes a noun or pronoun. It answers questions such as what kind, how many or which one: a practical idea, three rounds, this offer. Adjectives usually come before the noun, or after a linking verb (the idea is practical).

## Degrees of comparison

- Positive: describes one thing. This route is short.

- Comparative: compares two things, usually with "than". This route is shorter than that one.

- Superlative: picks the extreme among three or more, usually with "the". This is the shortest route.

Short adjectives add -er and -est (tall, taller, tallest). Adjectives ending in -y change to -ier and -iest (happy, happier, happiest). Longer adjectives use more and most (useful, more useful, most useful). Never combine both: not "more taller" and not "most easiest".

| Positive | Comparative | Superlative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| good | better | best |
| bad | worse | worst |
| far | farther / further | farthest / furthest |
| little (amount) | less | least |
| many / much | more | most |

## Order of adjectives

When you use several adjectives, the usual order is opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, then the noun. So we say a lovely small old wooden table, not a wooden old small lovely table.

Tip: Use fewer with countable nouns (fewer candidates) and less with uncountable ones (less time).

# Part 5: Adverbs: How, When, Where and How Much

An adverb tells us more about a verb, an adjective or another adverb. It answers how, when, where, how often or to what extent: She spoke clearly. They arrived early. The test was extremely tough.

## Common kinds

| Kind | Answers | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Manner | How? | carefully, politely, well |
| Time | When? | today, soon, already, yesterday |
| Place | Where? | here, outside, nearby, upstairs |
| Frequency | How often? | always, rarely, never, twice |
| Degree | How much? | very, quite, too, extremely |

## Forming adverbs

Many adverbs are formed by adding -ly to an adjective: quick, quickly; polite, politely; happy, happily. A few have the same form as the adjective: fast, hard, late, early. Watch for look-alikes with different meanings: hardly means "almost not" and lately means "recently". The adverb form of good is well.

## Where to place them

- Manner adverbs usually come after the verb or object: She replied politely.

- Frequency adverbs sit before the main verb but after be: He always checks. She is always punctual.

- Degree adverbs come before the word they modify: very tired, quite clearly.

Tip: Adjectives describe nouns, adverbs describe actions. Quick reply (noun described), reply quickly (action described).

# Part 6: Conjunctions and Interjections: Joining Ideas, Showing Feeling

A conjunction joins words, phrases or clauses. An interjection is a short exclamation that shows a sudden feeling. One is about logic and the other about emotion, so we treat them together.

## Three families of conjunctions

- Coordinating conjunctions join equal parts: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so. Use a comma before them when they join two full sentences: I prepared well, but I felt nervous.

- Subordinating conjunctions begin a dependent clause: because, although, if, unless, while, when, since, after, before, until, whereas. Because it rained, we stayed in.

- Correlative conjunctions come in pairs: both...and, either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also. Keep the structure balanced (parallel) on both sides.

## A mistake to avoid

Do not use two conjunctions to do one job. "Although it was late, but we finished" and "Because he was ill, so he left" are wrong. Choose one: Although it was late, we finished. Because he was ill, he left. Or: It was late, but we finished.

## Interjections

Words such as Wow!, Oops!, Hurray! and Ouch! express feeling. They are fine in friendly conversation and storytelling, but in professional emails and reports it is better to state the feeling in a full sentence: We are delighted to confirm your selection.

# Part 7: Same Word, Different Job: Putting It All Together

The most important idea in this module is that a word does not belong to one part of speech forever. Its job in the sentence decides what it is. Work can be a noun (My work is done) or a verb (I work in Pune). Fast can be an adjective (a fast laptop) or an adverb (she types fast).

## How to decide

- Ask what the word is doing here, not what it did in another sentence.

- If it names something, it is a noun. If it describes a noun, it is an adjective. If it describes an action, it is an adverb.

- Words like before, after and since can be prepositions (before the test) or conjunctions (before you leave), depending on whether a noun or a full clause follows.

Tip: In the exercises below, the word to identify is written in capital letters.

# Quick Recap

- Nouns name; pronouns replace nouns; verbs show action or state.

- Adjectives describe nouns; adverbs describe verbs, adjectives and other adverbs.

- Conjunctions join; interjections express feeling; prepositions (Module 3) link nouns to the rest of the sentence.

- Use I, he, she, we, they before the verb and me, him, her, us, them after a verb or preposition.

- After a linking verb, use an adjective: it sounds good, he looks tired.

- After a modal verb, use the base form: she can speak.

- Never double a comparison: taller, not more taller.

- Use one conjunction per link: although...(no but), because...(no so).

- A word's job comes from its place in the sentence, not from the word alone.
